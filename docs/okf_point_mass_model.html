Point-Mass Model

lob models a projectile as a point mass with three degrees of freedom: where it is, how fast it is moving, and how long it has been flying. No body, no orientation, no spin axis in the state vector. That is a severe simplification of a real bullet, and it is the right one. The point-mass trajectory is what the published reference solutions solve, so solving the same problem is what lets lob be checked against them. The things a point mass cannot represent, chiefly lift and the aerodynamics of a real body, enter later as measured corrections rather than as something guessed at mid-trajectory.

The state, and why distance is the independent variable

The state is a position, a velocity, and a time of flight. What makes the model interesting is the choice of independent variable: lob steps forward by down-range distance, not by time.

The reason is control. Stepping by distance means a single step parameter, expressed in yards of down-range progress, directly governs both accuracy and cost. A caller intercepting a target a few hundred yards out and a caller solving a multi-mile shot want different resolutions, and they get them from the same knob because the knob is already in the units they care about. Integrating in time would put the resolution choice in a place where the mapping from step size to trajectory accuracy depends on the shot itself.

Time of flight rides along inside the integration as a third component of the state, rather than being estimated afterward from the finished trajectory. It falls out of the stepping for free that way, and it stays consistent with the path that was actually taken rather than being reconstructed from it.

The equations

In the time domain, the model is the familiar one:

dp/dt = v
dv/dt = -k * |v - w| * (v - w) - Coriolis(v) + g
d(TOF)/dt = 1

where w is the wind vector, g is gravity, and k collects the Mach-dependent drag coefficient with the local air density. Drag acts on the velocity relative to the air, not relative to the ground, which is the entire reason wind enters the drag term and not as a separate nudge.

Because lob steps by down-range distance, everything divides through by the down-range velocity component:

dp/dx = v / vx
dv/dx = dv/dt / vx

and the time of flight, which accumulates in the time domain, becomes the reciprocal of down-range speed per unit distance. One expression covers all three state components, evaluated at whatever the current down-range position is.

Gravity and the rotation of the Earth are handled as adjustments to that frame rather than as separate physics. Gravity is rotated into the trajectory's own axes by the range inclination, so a shot downhill and the same shot uphill fall in the direction the shooter is actually facing. The Coriolis term is the standard coupling of the three axes for a rotating observer:

dvx/dt -= vy * 2w cos(lat) sin(az) + vz * 2w sin(lat)
dvy/dt += vx * 2w cos(lat) sin(az) + vz * 2w cos(lat) cos(az)
dvz/dt += vx * 2w sin(lat) - vy * 2w cos(lat) cos(az)

Only the latitude and azimuth of the shot are involved, and the coefficients collapse to three scalars that get computed once when the context is built rather than on every step.

Where the ballistic coefficient is folded in depends on how the drag curve was supplied. A standard curve carries it, scaled into the curve itself, so the density scaling applied each step stays a pure function of the air. A custom table or a velocity-band curve already embeds it. Either way the per-step scaling only has to answer "how thick is the air right here", which keeps the two concerns from competing for the same coefficient.

When the projectile stops advancing

A step-by-distance integrator has a degenerate case: if the projectile is no longer making down-range progress, there is no distance left to step through and dividing by its down-range velocity is undefined. That happens when a lofted shot has passed its apex and is falling nearly straight down.

When down-range velocity becomes nonpositive, DsDxCore zeroes the derivative contribution and the step helpers hold velocity at zero; this prevents continued descent in the distance-based integration rather than producing a division by zero. The behavior differs by caller: the direct angle-solving helpers FireToTarget and SolveAngle treat a non-advancing state as unreachable and return "no answer". By contrast, LobSolve may repeat after FastSolveStep zeroes velocity before its terminal checks fire in some cases, so it does not guarantee termination for stalled progress; LobSolveInverse calls LobSolve first and therefore inherits that behavior. A shot that will not arrive is not always reported as unreachable through a single common path.

Two step variants

The equations above admit two ways to treat the air, and lob ships both because they trade against each other.

The fast variant assumes the air is the air at the firing site, for the whole flight. A bullet that stays in roughly one atmosphere sees almost no error from this, and the per-step cost drops to almost nothing because nothing about the air needs recomputing. This is the default for forward solves and for finding a zero.

The lapse variant recomputes density and speed of sound each step, projecting the projectile's climb onto the standard lapse rate, so a long shot that gains thousands of feet of altitude flies through correctly thinning air. It costs real work per step.

Inverse solving picks between them per range, on the basis of how much climb that particular shot needs. Shots that stay low take the cheap path; the long ones pay. The two are not guaranteed to agree when altitude-dependent drag or speed-of-sound corrections are nonzero, so the choice is about how hard the shot is and the tradeoff being made, not a correctness difference in all conditions.

Assumptions

Stated plainly, because they bound what the solutions mean:

  • Point mass. No lift. The spin-related effects that a point mass cannot represent are applied afterward as corrections: the aerodynamic jump enters the launch angle, and spin drift enters the lateral deflection.
  • Flat Earth over the horizon a trajectory actually covers. Altitude-dependent air density comes from the lapse variant, not from a curved gravity field.
  • Uniform wind. One vector for the whole flight. Wind shear along the trajectory is not part of the point-mass model.
