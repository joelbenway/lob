Angle Solver Mechanics

lob has exactly one routine that answers "what launch angle makes this shot go there?". It serves both features that need that answer, and it is the same routine for both, down to the tolerance, the bounds, and the iteration policy. This page explains the problem it solves, how it works, why it is shared rather than duplicated, and what backs it up.

The problem

Two features need a launch angle solved for:

  • Zero. The shooter picks a distance and an impact height. lob returns the mechanical launch angle that puts the bullet through the optic axis at that distance. This is computed once, when the solver context is built, and stored as the context's zero angle.
  • Inverse. The shooter asks for the adjustment that moves impact onto a target at some range. lob returns that adjustment relative to the zero angle, because that is what a shooter actually needs: the dope for the gun they just zeroed.

Both reduce to the same residual function. Fire a trajectory from a candidate angle, integrate it out to the requested range, and measure the miss:

f(θ) = height_at_range(θ) - optic_height - desired_impact_height

The answer is the root of f. A zero is the root that lands on the optic axis; an inverse adjustment is the root that lands on the target, minus the zero.

Historically a ballistics library grows two implementations here, one per call site, and the two drift subtly apart. That drift is the whole reason this is one routine: a rifle zeroed by one solver and adjusted by the other wears the difference between them as a bias.

The zero angle is a number, not a procedure

The zero comes out of this solver when the caller does not supply one, and is stored on the context as a single angle in minutes of arc. If the caller already knows the zero, the build bounds-checks it and returns, skipping the search entirely. That is the shape the whole design wants, and it is worth being explicit about why.

A zero describes a gun, not a shot. Once you know the distance and the impact height, the mechanical angle that sends the bullet through the optic axis is a fixed property of the rifle, the ammunition, and the conditions. Nothing about a later forward solve or inverse solve changes it. So the search runs once, when the context is built, and what stays behind is the answer rather than the machinery that found it.

Everything downstream then reads that number. A forward solve launches from it. An inverse solve reports adjustments relative to it. The angle solver uses it as the seed for every range in a table. None of them pays for the search again, which is why a context that has been built is cheap to keep solving against for as long as the caller likes.

The number is also portable in the useful sense. It is an angle, so it does not care what units the shooter entered them in, and it does not depend on the range being asked about. A caller who zeroed in SI units, who queries in yards, and who hands the context to a colleague solving in meters is passing around one number that means the same thing to all of them.

There is a second saving available. A caller who already knows their zero, from a dope book or a previous session, can state it directly. The build then bounds-checks it and returns, skipping the seed and the search altogether. For a shooter working from published data that is the normal path, and it makes the zero a pure input rather than a computation.

How it works

The solver alternates between two cheap steps until it settles.

Fire and measure. Integrate a full trajectory from the candidate angle out to the range, and return the residual f. The Heun integrator supplies the step, against the drag curve the context already carries.

One geometric step. Correct the angle in closed form. If the shot at range R landed f too low, raise elevation by approximately atan(f / R):

θ_next = atan( tan(θ) - f / R )

This is the flat-fire correction, and it is why the iteration converges quickly: it already accounts for most of the error, and each pass only has to trim the remainder.

Settle or give up. Repeat until the angle stops moving by more than the tolerance, then return. The search is bounded to a physically sensible range of launch angles, and the iteration count is capped, so the routine always terminates.

Honest failure. A shot that cannot reach the requested range at all (it stalls out, or runs past its time or speed limits) has no residual to speak of, and neither does a root outside the bounds. In both cases the solver returns "no answer" rather than a plausible-looking garbage angle. At build time that becomes an explicit unreachable-zero error; in an inverse solve it truncates the batch at the range that failed, so a caller never receives a fabricated adjustment for the ranges past it.

Two entry points exist, differing only in which density the integration assumes as the projectile climbs: one holds the firing-site density, the other recomputes density per step. Inverse solving picks between them based on how far the shot has to climb. Everything else about the search, including the shared tolerance and bounds, is identical.

Seeds carry intent

Iteration needs a first guess, and each call site knows something better than "zero" about where the answer lies.

  • Zero seeds from the vacuum parabola. With no drag at all, the small-angle approximation for a level launch is 0.5 * g * R / v² (the exact low-angle form is ½ asin(gR/v²)). That approximation is the closed-form answer to the simplest possible version of the problem, so it is a principled place to start. It is clamped to the search bounds before use.
  • Inverse seeds from the forward solve. A forward solve already reports how far the shot lands off at that range. Applying the same one geometric step to the zero angle with that error corrects the zero into a near-miss guess for the target, which the iteration then refines.

One subtlety is deliberately inside the trajectory rather than at each call site: aerodynamic jump is added to the launch angle when the initial velocity is built. So the angle the solver searches over is the mechanical angle the shooter thinks in, and both the zero and the inverse adjustments inherit jump identically. Neither caller has to remember to apply it, which is one more way the two paths cannot drift.

Why sharing matters

Consistency. The zero a rifle is zeroed with and the adjustments that assume that zero come out of identical tolerance, bounds, iteration policy, and jump handling. Split the routine and each of those becomes a place the two can disagree.

Maintenance. A fix to convergence behavior, a change to the bounds, a new failure mode: it applies to both features at once, and one guard inside the shared routine is a smaller and safer diff than the same guard repeated at every call site.

What backs it up

The solver tests pin the properties the design depends on:

  • Round-trip. Firing at a solved angle reproduces the height that angle was solved for, so the residual means what the formula says it means.
  • Builder and solver agree. The zero the builder computes is identical to calling the shared routine directly, so the builder adds no drift of its own on the way in.
  • Iteration earns its keep. The refined answer agrees with the single geometric step, including when aerodynamic jump is nonzero. If the iteration ever started drifting away from the closed-form answer it would be inventing results the one-step physics does not support.
  • The two density variants agree where both are valid, so choosing between them per range is a speed choice rather than an accuracy choice.
